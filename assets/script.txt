The first time you see Batman, you think he has come to save you.

Then he grabs your radio and says three words.

Don't open it.

Across the street, a little girl is trapped inside a school bus. The windows are fogging up. She's pounding on the glass. And the door is locked.

Batman is telling you not to open it.

Because somewhere in Gotham, the Joker is watching. And the bus is part of his joke.

Six days earlier, you were just a rookie cop who still believed a badge meant you could protect people.

Your name is Alex Reyes. You grew up in Gotham, in an apartment above your father's repair shop. Every night, your father locked the front door, checked it twice, and left a small lamp burning in the window.

You once asked him why he bothered. A lamp wasn't going to stop a criminal.

No, he said. But it tells you which window is home.

The night you graduate from the academy, he gives you an old flashlight. The metal is scratched, the switch is loose, and the beam flickers when you shake it.

You laugh. He doesn't.

Keep it, he says. Just in case the lights go out.

Your first partner is Officer Mara Bennett. She's been with the Gotham police long enough to remember when the station windows weren't reinforced.

On your first patrol, you pass the ruins of a bank. Half the front wall is gone. Reporters crowd behind a barricade, waiting for someone to say the Joker's name.

Mara walks past them and stops beside a bank employee sitting on the curb. The woman keeps staring at her hands.

Her brother worked inside. No one has told her whether he made it out.

You want to chase the story. Mara hands you a bottle of water and asks you to stay with the woman while she finds out.

Twenty minutes later, Mara comes back. The woman's brother is alive. He was taken to a hospital across town.

There are no cameras when she tells her. The reporters are still pointing their lenses at the hole in the wall.

That night, you see the Bat-Signal for the first time. A great black shape against the clouds. For a moment, even the traffic seems to slow.

You look up. Mara keeps driving.

You ask if Batman will catch the Joker.

Maybe, she says. But even if he does, somebody still has to answer the phones.

On your third night, a robbery turns into a chase through the Narrows. By the time you arrive, the robber is hanging upside down from a fire escape, wrapped in a cable. A stolen bag lies beneath him, untouched.

You look up. A cape disappears over the edge of a roof.

Your first real glimpse of Batman lasts less than a second.

The robber is yelling that the bat broke his arm. The paramedics say his arm is fine. His pride is a different story.

You almost smile. Mara doesn't.

She points to the people watching from their windows. They're not watching the robber. They're watching the roof.

In Gotham, she says, even the good news makes people afraid to look up.

On your fourth day, a package arrives at the station. No return address. A bright red ribbon around a plain white box.

The desk sergeant clears the room and calls the bomb squad. Inside, they find a cheap wind-up toy. A little policeman with a painted smile.

Its key has been turned all the way. When the specialist releases it, the toy marches across the table, bumps into a wall, and falls over.

Taped to its back is a message.

How many cops does it take to save Gotham?

Underneath, in red ink: Let's find out.

By evening, the toy is in evidence. Everyone at the station is pretending they aren't thinking about it.

Mara tells you to go home and sleep.

You call your father instead. He says the shop lights have been flickering again. You promise to stop by after your shift and fix the loose switch.

Then the line goes dead.

On your sixth night, Gotham loses power.

Not all at once. First a few blocks. Then another. Traffic lights go black. Storefronts disappear. Far away, the Bat-Signal clicks off halfway through its sweep, leaving an empty circle in the clouds.

Calls flood the radio. A bridge is jammed. An alarm is ringing at a hospital. Somewhere near Robinson Park, people report a bus stopped in the middle of the street.

You and Mara are closest.

When you arrive, the bus is empty except for one child. She's sitting in the back, wearing a yellow raincoat, holding a small flashlight against the window.

The driver is outside, shaking. He says a man in a purple coat stopped the bus, sent everyone else away, and locked the girl inside.

A telephone rings in the driver's pocket.

He says the man gave it to him. He was told to answer when a police officer arrived.

You tell him not to touch it. Mara steps between him and the bus and calls for the bomb squad. The little girl sees your uniform and starts banging on the window.

The phone keeps ringing.

Mara answers on speaker.

A man's voice asks whether the city is enjoying the dark. You know that voice, even though you have never heard it in person. Every cop in Gotham does.

The Joker says the bus door is connected to a device. Open it, and something goes off. He doesn't say what. He doesn't say where.

Then he laughs and hangs up.

You look at the girl. She presses a hand against the glass. Her flashlight slips from her fingers and rolls beneath a seat.

The bomb squad is on its way, but the roads are blocked. The driver says the girl has been alone for nearly half an hour. The windows are getting harder to see through.

You take a step toward the door.

Something lands on the roof above you. A heavy shape. A cape settles against the brick.

Batman drops into the street.

For a second, nobody moves. The driver steps back. Even Mara looks up.

You start explaining, but Batman has already seen the wire near the door. He takes your radio.

Don't open it.

Then he tells dispatch what he's found. His voice is low, steady. He asks Mara to move everyone back. You expect him to open the bus himself.

He doesn't.

He checks the street, the rooftops, the abandoned cars. The Joker wanted an audience. Batman wants to know where the audience is hiding.

The phone rings again.

This time Batman answers.

The Joker sounds delighted. He says there are two problems tonight. A child on a bus, and a room full of patients at a hospital whose backup power is about to fail.

He tells Batman he can save one, but not both.

Batman asks which hospital.

The Joker laughs. Find out.

The call ends.

Batman looks at Mara. She has already requested confirmation from dispatch. A moment later, the report comes back. One hospital has lost backup power, and emergency crews are responding. No one yet knows whether the Joker caused it.

Batman looks at the child. Then at the far end of the street.

You think he's about to leave her.

He tells Mara the bomb squad is two blocks away. He points out the wire and asks her to relay everything he's seen. Then he turns to you.

Stay with her.

That's it. No speech. No promise.

He fires a line into the darkness and disappears over the rooftops.

You stare after him. The girl is still at the window.

The Joker wanted you to see this as a choice between two lives. Batman left a team at the bus and went where he might be needed next. You don't know whether it will be enough.

The girl starts crying. You can't hear her through the glass, but you can see her shoulders shaking.

You remember your father's shop. The little lamp in the window.

You pull out the old flashlight. The beam flickers. You tap the switch, point it toward the bus, and raise your free hand.

The girl sees you. She wipes her face and raises her hand too.

You keep the light on her window. When it flickers, you tap it again.

Mara stands beside you, speaking to the arriving specialists. The bomb squad examines the wire while firefighters keep the street clear. Nobody asks you to be a hero. They ask you to stay where the girl can see you.

So you do.

The specialist gives Mara a signal. The device has been made safe. A firefighter opens the door, and the girl climbs down into her mother's arms.

You hear her crying now. So is her mother.

The flashlight goes out in your hand.

At the hospital, the emergency crew restores power. Later, the radio reports that Batman found the Joker nearby. There is no dramatic announcement, just officers requesting transport and a medic. The Joker is in custody. Tonight, at least.

By morning, the city is arguing about Batman again. Some people say he should never have left the bus. Others say the hospital needed him more. On television, the reporters show the Joker's grin and the black sky where the Bat-Signal went dark.

Nobody shows the little girl waving through the window. Nobody mentions Mara or the specialists who opened the door. That's all right. Her mother knows their names.

At the end of your shift, you go to your father's shop. The lights are back on, but the lamp in the window is dark.

Your father is asleep in his chair. You don't wake him. You set the old flashlight on the counter, open your toolbox, and fix the loose switch on the lamp.

When it glows, your father opens one eye.

Long night? he asks.

You look outside. Across the street, a delivery driver is unlocking a shop. A bus passes. Somewhere above the buildings, the clouds begin to turn gray.

The Joker will escape again someday. In Gotham, everyone knows that. Batman will be there when he does.

And someone will still have to answer the phones. Someone will have to stand beside the people waiting for news.

Your father looks at the flashlight on the counter. The beam is dead.

I can fix it, he says.

You smile. I know.

If you were an ordinary cop in Gotham, would you stay? And which world should we enter next?
